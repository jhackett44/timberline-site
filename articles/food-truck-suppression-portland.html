Hood Suppression Requirements for Portland Food Trucks

4 min read
Published by Timberline Fire Systems
Portland, Oregon

Portland has one of the largest food truck scenes in the country, with more than 500 trucks operating across the metro at any given time. What a lot of first-time operators don't realize is that a food truck with cooking equipment under a hood is held to the exact same fire suppression standard as a full-service restaurant. Here's what that actually means for your truck.

The short answer: NFPA 96 applies to mobile kitchens too

NFPA 96 doesn't distinguish between a building and a vehicle. If your truck has a Type I hood over grease-producing cooking equipment, wet chemical fire suppression is required, and that system needs to be inspected on the same semi-annual schedule as any fixed commercial kitchen.

Oregon and Portland both enforce this. A mobile food unit isn't exempt just because it moves.

What's different about a food truck inspection?

The core inspection is the same: nozzles, fusible links, agent cylinder, pull station, hood clearance. But a few things are unique to a mobile kitchen and worth knowing before your technician shows up.

Manual actuation distance

For fixed kitchens, NFPA 96 recommends a manual pull station be located 10 to 20 feet from the hazard. Mobile applications follow the same range, but in a truck's tight footprint that distance often has to be measured and verified carefully since there's a lot less room to work with than a full kitchen.

Fuel connections. Most trucks run on propane rather than natural gas, and some run on electric. Whatever the setup, the interlock between your suppression system and your fuel source has to be verified as part of the inspection, same as it would be in a building, just with propane specific components in play.

Access and layout. Every truck is laid out a little differently, which means every inspection is a little different too. A technician who hasn't worked mobile kitchens before can find this genuinely disorienting. It's one of the reasons food truck operators report a harder time finding someone willing to do the job at all.

Do you need a different permit for the suppression system?

The suppression system itself isn't permitted separately from your mobile food unit permit, but your inspection report is part of what keeps that permit valid. If your suppression system lapses on its inspection schedule, it can affect your ability to operate legally, the same way it would put a restaurant's operating permit at risk.

Keep your signed inspection reports on file. If you ever get asked for proof of compliance during a permit renewal or a health inspection, that report is what you hand over.

What about trucks that share a commissary kitchen?

Some food trucks prep at a shared commissary and only do light cooking on the truck itself. If your truck doesn't have a hood over grease-producing equipment, wet chemical suppression may not apply to you at all. This is worth confirming rather than assuming either way, since the requirement is based on your equipment and hood setup, not on the fact that you operate a truck.

Why this market gets overlooked

Most suppression companies are built around fixed commercial kitchens and treat mobile units as an inconvenience. Tight spaces, unconventional layouts, and the fact that a truck might not be parked in the same spot twice all make food trucks more work per visit than a stationary restaurant.

Bottom line

That's exactly why so many food truck operators report trouble getting a technician to call them back at all.
