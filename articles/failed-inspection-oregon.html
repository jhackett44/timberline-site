What Happens If Your Kitchen Hood Suppression System Fails Inspection in Oregon?

5 min read
Published by Timberline Fire Systems
Portland, Oregon

A failed kitchen hood suppression inspection is not the end of the world. But it does require immediate attention. Here's what a failed inspection actually means, what your legal obligations are, and how to get back into compliance as quickly as possible.

First, what does "failed" actually mean?

There's no single pass/fail stamp on a suppression system inspection. What a technician documents are deficiencies, specific components or conditions that don't meet the requirements of NFPA 96 or your local fire code.

Deficiencies fall into two general categories. Some are minor and can be corrected on the spot during the same visit. A clogged nozzle, a missing cap, a fusible link that needs replacement. Others are more significant and require follow-up work before the system can be considered compliant. A discharged cylinder, a damaged pull station, a system that's been physically altered without proper documentation.

The inspection report documents every deficiency found, what was corrected during the visit, and what still needs to be addressed.

What are you legally required to do after a failed inspection?

Under NFPA 96 and Portland Fire Code, deficiencies must be corrected within a reasonable timeframe. For serious deficiencies that compromise the system's ability to activate, that timeframe can be very short.

Important

Your signed inspection report is a legal document. If a fire occurs after a deficiency was documented and not corrected, that report becomes part of the liability picture. Your insurer will see it. Your fire marshal may ask for it. Ignoring documented deficiencies is not an option.

Can you keep operating with deficiencies?

It depends on the severity. Minor deficiencies that don't compromise the system's core function generally don't result in an immediate shutdown order. Your technician will note them, you'll have them corrected, and you'll get a follow-up inspection to confirm.

Serious deficiencies are a different story. If your system cannot reasonably be expected to activate in a fire event, Portland Fire and Rescue has the authority to require immediate corrective action or issue an order affecting your operations.

The honest truth

Most restaurant operators never face a shutdown over suppression deficiencies because most deficiencies are caught and corrected during the inspection visit itself. A technician who catches a problem and fixes it on the spot is doing their job. A technician who rubber stamps a system with real issues is a liability.

What does the correction process look like?

For deficiencies corrected on the spot, your inspection report documents both the deficiency and the corrective action taken. You're compliant as of that visit.

For deficiencies requiring follow-up, you'll typically need:

  • A return visit from your technician to complete the repair or replacement
  • An updated inspection report documenting the corrective action
  • In some cases, a re-inspection to confirm the system is fully operational

The timeline depends on parts availability and the nature of the deficiency. Most common repairs, cylinder recharges, cartridge replacements, pull station repairs, can be completed within a few days.

What about your insurance?

This is where a lot of restaurant operators get caught off guard. Your commercial property and general liability policies almost certainly contain language about maintaining your fire suppression system in proper working order.

A documented deficiency that goes uncorrected creates a gap in that coverage. If a fire occurs while a known deficiency exists, your insurer has grounds to dispute the claim. The inspection report you file is the paper trail that either protects you or works against you.

Best practice

Keep every inspection report. Keep documentation of every corrective action. If your insurer ever asks for maintenance records, you want a clean file going back as far as possible.

How do you avoid deficiencies in the first place?

Most suppression system deficiencies are predictable and preventable. Fusible links degrade over time and must be replaced semi-annually. Nozzle caps get lost or damaged. Cylinders lose pressure. Agent cartridges have a service life.

A good technician catches these things before they become deficiencies. The best way to avoid a failed inspection is to stay on schedule with your semi-annual inspections and work with a technician who is thorough rather than fast.

The bottom line

A deficiency on your inspection report is a problem to solve, not a reason to panic. Get it corrected quickly, keep the documentation, and stay on your semi-annual schedule. That's the entire compliance playbook.

If you're behind on inspections or you've inherited a system with unknown history, the right move is to get a technician out as soon as possible. Better to find the deficiencies yourself than to have a fire marshal or insurance adjuster find them for you.
